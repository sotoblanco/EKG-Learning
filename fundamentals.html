Section 1

What is an EKG?

The Electrocardiogram (EKG or ECG) is a graphical recording of the electrical activity of the heart over time. It is captured using surface electrodes placed on the skin of the chest and limbs.

Before the heart muscle actually contracts (mechanical activity), an electrical signal propagates through it. The EKG "listens" to this electrical wave from different angles, allowing us to diagnose arrhythmias, infarctions, blocks, and hypertrophy.

The Golden Rule of EKG:

Electrical activity precedes mechanical contraction.

(The EKG shows the electricity, not the actual pumping of blood!)

💡 Clinical Pearl

Always treat the patient, not the paper. If the EKG shows a flatline (asystole) but the patient is sitting up and talking to you, check your lead connections! Artifacts and disconnected leads happen frequently.

Section 2

The EKG Paper

EKG paper is a standardized grid. Understanding this grid is mandatory for calculating heart rate and diagnosing blocks. The horizontal axis represents time, and the vertical axis represents voltage (amplitude).

Graphic scaled for screen visibility. Standard speed = 25mm/s.

Select a box size to see its standard measurements in time and voltage.

  • Standard Paper Speed: 25 mm/sec.
  • Standard Calibration: 10 mm / 1 mV. Look for the calibration box at the start of the EKG (it should be 2 large boxes high).

Memorize this:

1 Small Box = 0.04 seconds

1 Large Box = 5 small boxes = 0.20 seconds

The 6 Limb Leads (Vertical Plane)

  • I, II, III: Bipolar leads forming Einthoven's Triangle.
  • aVR, aVL, aVF: Augmented unipolar leads.
  • aVR is the "lonely" lead. Everything usually looks upside down in aVR because it looks at the heart from the right shoulder.

The 6 Precordial Leads (Horizontal Plane)

  • Placed directly on the chest.
  • V1, V2: Right side of the septum.
  • V3, V4: Anterior wall of Left Ventricle.
  • V5, V6: Lateral wall of Left Ventricle.

Section 4

The Normal EKG Waveform

A single cardiac cycle produces a predictable series of waves. Hover over the points on the graph below to understand what each electrical segment represents anatomically.

Interactive Idealized Normal Sinus Rhythm Complex.

P Wave

Atrial depolarization (contraction).
Normal: < 120ms duration (3 small boxes), < 2.5mm amplitude. Upright in II, inverted in aVR.

PR Interval

From start of P to start of QRS. Represents delay at the AV node allowing ventricles to fill.
Normal: 120-200ms (3-5 small boxes).

QRS Complex

Ventricular depolarization.
Normal: < 120ms (< 3 small boxes). Q = first negative, R = first positive, S = negative after R.

ST Segment

End of S to start of T. Normally isoelectric (flat on baseline).
Elevation = Transmural Injury (STEMI). Depression = Subendocardial ischemia.

T Wave

Ventricular repolarization. Usually upright where the QRS is upright.

QT Interval

Total ventricular activity time. Varies with heart rate. Must use corrected QT (QTc, Bazett formula). Prolonged QTc → risk of Torsades de Pointes.

Section 5

Systematic Approach

Never jump straight to the ST segments! Always read an EKG using the same systematic order to avoid missing subtle diagnoses. Click each step below.

1. Rate ▼

Normal: 60 - 100 bpm.

Rule of 300 (Regular Rhythm): 300 / (Number of large boxes between R waves).
Sequence: 300 - 150 - 100 - 75 - 60 - 50

10 Second Rule (Irregular Rhythm): Count total R waves in a 10-second strip and multiply by 6.

2. Rhythm ▼
  • Is it regular or irregular?
  • Is there a P wave before every QRS?
  • Is there a QRS after every P wave?
  • Are the P waves upright in II, III, and aVF? (Indicates normal Sinus Rhythm origin).
3. Axis ▼

The general direction of electrical travel. Look at Leads I and aVF.

  • 👍 Lead I (+) & 👍 aVF (+) = Normal Axis (Two thumbs up!)
  • 👍 Lead I (+) & 👍 aVF (-) = Left Axis Deviation (Thumbs leaving each other)
  • 👍 Lead I (-) & 👍 aVF (+) = Right Axis Deviation (Thumbs reaching toward each other)
4. Intervals ▼
  • PR Interval: Is it > 200ms? (1st Degree AV Block). Is it short? (WPW syndrome).
  • QRS Width: Is it > 120ms? (Bundle Branch Block or ventricular origin).
  • QTc: Is it > 440ms (men) / 460ms (women)?
5. Hypertrophy ▼

Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH) Voltage Criteria (Sokolow-Lyon):

S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 ≥ 35 mm

6. Ischemia / Infarction ▼

Look for ST segment changes, T wave inversions, and pathological Q waves.

  • Ischemia: Symmetrical T wave inversion, ST depression.
  • Injury (Acute Infarct): ST segment elevation (STEMI).
  • Necrosis (Old Infarct): Pathologic Q waves (deep and wide).

Section 6

Common Artifacts

Not everything that wiggles is a dangerous arrhythmia. Recognizing artifacts prevents unnecessary panics and treatments.

Muscle Tremor (Somatic)

/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\

Patient is cold, shivering, or has Parkinson's. The baseline is fuzzy and erratic. Fix: Warm the patient, ask them to relax.

Wandering Baseline

    ——¯¯——         __——

The entire EKG tracing waves up and down. Usually caused by breathing (respiratory variation) or loose electrodes. Fix: Secure electrodes, ensure good skin contact.

60 Hz AC Interference

||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||||

Thick, perfectly regular fuzzy baseline caused by electrical interference from nearby equipment or lights. Fix: Unplug non-essential equipment, use EKG machine filters.

Limb Lead Reversal

Lead I looks inverted!

Most commonly Right Arm and Left Arm are swapped. P, QRS, and T are all completely upside down in Lead I. Fix: Check the wire labels and replace properly!
